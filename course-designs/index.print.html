CIS 115 Introduction to Computer Science

Course Description

A survey of the discipline of Computing Science and its interaction with other disciplines, incorporating historical development, theories, and tools of Computing Science (algorithm design and programming). Topics include: mechanical and digital computers, computational thinking, security, simulation, data science, web technologies, and more. Students will be able to explore real-world problems within computer science and research current topics.

Credit Hours

2 credits

Course Overview

This course is an introduction to the history, fundamental theories, basic programming, and research areas within Computing Science and its related disciplines. Computing Science has a direct impact on many facets of daily life, but very few of us ever take the time to wonder how it all works. This course aims to fill that void.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Describe the history of Computing Science and list some of the important devices, innovations, and people that got us to where we are today.
  2. Relate Computing Science to a variety of other disciplines and describe how they are interconnected with each other.
  3. Apply and use Computing Science tools and techniques to solve real-world problems.
  4. Research and learn about new ideas and areas in Computing Science and share those ideas with others, all without direct guidance.
  5. Develop a personal understanding of how Computing Science affects your own life.
  6. Understand many different subject areas within Computing Science and how they are changing our understanding of the field.

Course Prerequisites

Corequisite: CIS 116

Required Textbooks and Equipment

  • “The Pattern on the Stone: The Simple Ideas that Make Computers Work” by W. Daniel Hillis. ISBN 046502596X, newer version is also available and will work fine.
  • “Nine Algorithms That Changed the Future: The Ingenious Ideas That Drive Today’s Computers” by John MacCormick. ISBN 0691158193
  • The following are provided:
    • “CS Zero: An Introduction to Computing Science”
    • “Introduction to Python”

Grading Policy

Grading Modality: Graded (A, B, C, D, F, I)

Course Topics

  • The history of Computing Science and early computing machines
  • Computational representation: binary and Boolean logic, number systems, a simplified floating-point format, and register-level state
  • Computational thinking, application of programming, and algorithm design
  • The history and technology behind the internet and how it affects our world
  • Traditional Computing Science areas such as artificial intelligence, human-computer interaction, high performance computing, big data, robotics, and more
  • Cybersecurity in a modern, interconnected world
  • Other disciplines and how they relate to Computing Science

  • 25% - Quizzes (Lowest two scores are dropped)
  • 20% - Topic Research and Wiki Article

CIS 116 – Introduction to Programming

Course Description

An introduction to programming by exploring core concepts such as variables, conditionals, iteration, basic data structures, functions, and classes. Hands-on activities to learn and apply concepts to real-world problems and ideas. Students will gain a solid foundation in programming, setting the stage for further study and professional use.

Course Overview

This course serves as a primary introduction to modern programming by exploring core concepts such as variables, conditionals, iteration, and functions. To accomplish this goal, we will do several hands-on learning activities. While doing so, we will try to relate the concepts we are learning to real-world problems and ideas. Students will gain a solid foundation in programming, setting the stage for further study and professional use.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Understand the basics of computer programming and the significance of programming in the modern world.
  2. Set up a programming development environment.
  3. Write, debug, and execute computer programs.
  4. Read, interpret, and modify existing computer programs.
  5. Utilize a programming language’s built-in functions, libraries, and modules.
  6. Understand and apply the basic structures of programming, including variables, data types, operators, control structures, loops, and functions.
  7. Engage with basic object-oriented programming concepts like classes and objects. -8.Create basic real-world applications using a computer programming language.

  • Create and use functions to organize and modularize code, applying principles of reusability and abstraction.
  • Write programs that handle basic user input and display output, including file I/O operations.

CIS 225 - Foundations of Computer Networks

Course Description

Survey of computer networking fundamentals, including 7-layer OSI model, common networking protocols, socket-based network programming, network security and encryption, and network performance and reliability basics.

Credit Hours

1 credit

Course Overview

This course will introduce students to the core concepts of network communication between computing systems. Students will learn about the 7-layer OSI network model and how common networking protocols such as TCP/IP, HTTP, DHCP, DNS, and more work to form the foundation of the modern internet. Students will explore basic socket-based network programming, discuss security and encryption of network communications, and explore concepts related to network performance and reliability.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Explain the 7-level OSI model, and discuss where various protocols sit within that model
  2. Describe how data is encapsulated into packets for transmission
  3. Construct a working socket-based networked program such as a chat server
  4. Differentiate between common networking protocols
  5. Assess security vulnerabilities and analyze the security of a network connection
  6. Describe the distributed structure of the internet
  7. Examine network performance and reliability metrics

CIS 260 - Foundations of Relational Databases

Course Description

This 1‑credit hour course introduces students to Structured Query Language (SQL) for retrieving and analyzing data in relational databases. Students will learn to write basic queries to select, filter, summarize, and combine data from multiple tables using hands‑on exercises and real‑world examples.

Credit Hours

1 credit

Course Overview

The objective of this course is to provide students with the foundations to understand simple relational databases. With this knowledge, students will be able to understand and write SQL queries to find, organize, and summarize information from real-world datasets.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Explain the purpose of SQL and how relational databases are used to store and retrieve structured data.
  2. Write basic SQL queries using (but not limited to) SELECT, FROM, and WHERE to retrieve specific data from a relational database.
  3. Sort and filter query results using ORDER BY, comparison operators, and logical operators.
  4. Summarize data using aggregate functions, such as COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX, with GROUP BY.
  5. Combine data from multiple tables using basic JOIN operations.
  6. Write SQL queries that modify data in an existing database, such as INSERT, UPDATE, and DELETE
  7. Use programmable objects to create meaningful transformations of data, such as VIEWS and table expressions.
  8. Employ advanced SQL techniques, like sub queries, to answer more complex questions
  9. Interpret query results to answer practical, real‑world questions.
  10. Demonstrate understanding of basic relational database design

Course Prerequisites

None.

CIS 300 - Data and Program Structures

Course Description

A study of common data and program structures together with associated algorithms. Topics include interfaces, design patterns, and common data structures (such as arrays, stacks, queues, lists, trees, graphs, hash tables), as well as common algorithms that utilize these structures. Students will gain experience with both use and implementation of these structures and algorithms using a modern programming language. Students will also learn the various tradeoffs involving performance and software maintainability in data structure choice.

Credit Hours

3 credits

Course Overview

This course examines the fundamental data structures, algorithms, and software design principles used in modern computing. Students will learn to analyze, implement, and apply common data structures such as arrays, stacks, queues, lists, trees, graphs, hash tables and more, along with the algorithms that operate on them. Through hands-on programming experiences, students will explore interfaces, abstraction, and design patterns while developing solutions to computational problems and data structures. Emphasis is placed on evaluating algorithm efficiency, understanding performance tradeoffs, and selecting appropriate data structures to create efficient and maintainable software.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Write programs which implement and use abstract and reference-type data structures, including, but not limited to:

    • Strings and string parsing (and related structures like string builders)
    • Lists and arrays
    • Linked lists
    • Stacks
    • Queues and priority queues
    • Trees (including, but not limited to binary search trees, AVL trees, and tries)
    • Sets
    • Hash tables
    • Dictionaries
    • Heaps
    • Graphs

  2. Select and use appropriate data structures to solve real-world problems.

  3. Understand and explain how the performance of a program may change based on the data structure chosen.

  4. Implement and apply algorithms for searching, sorting, and manipulating a data structure.

  5. Understand and use fundamental algorithms for data structures listed in SLO #1, including, but not limited to:

    • Standard sorting/search algorithms (e.g., insertion sort, merge sort, quicksort, etc.)
    • Advanced sorting/searching algorithms (e.g., Dijkstra’s algorithm, greedy algorithms, bucket sort, radix sort, etc.)
    • Tree algorithms
    • Graph algorithms
    • Recursive and iterative algorithms

  6. Analyze performance differences and complexity between various algorithms and data structures as outlined in SLO #1.

  7. Utilize advanced object-oriented concepts, such as generics, interfaces, indexers, and enumerators, to implement or partially implement data structures.

Course Prerequisites

The prerequisites for this course are CIS 200 and either DISCRETE MATH 4 or MATH 220. A grade of a C or better is required for each prerequisite.

CIS 301 - Logical Foundations of Programming

Course Description

Logical formalisms used to model and reason about computer systems. Propositional and predicate logic, syntax, semantics, and proof theory; soundness and completeness issues. Mathematical induction. Program verification: invariants and program logics.

Credit Hours

2 credits

Course Overview

This course provides an introduction to formal logic and its application to mathematical reasoning and software correctness. Students will learn to represent and analyze logical statements using propositional and predicate logic, evaluate arguments and proofs, and apply formal reasoning techniques such as equivalence laws, deduction rules, contradiction, and induction. Topics include truth tables, sets, quantifiers, logical proofs, and the translation of natural language into formal symbolic expressions. The course also examines the use of logic in computing, with an emphasis on reasoning about program correctness through specifications, preconditions, postconditions, invariants, loops, functions, and program termination. By the end of the course, students will be able to apply formal methods to analyze logical arguments and verify the correctness of computational systems.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

  1. Represent and analyze logical statements using formal symbolic notation, including propositional and predicate logic.
  2. Translate between natural language and formal logical expressions to model statements, arguments, and relationships precisely.
  3. Evaluate the validity of logical arguments using truth tables, equivalence laws, counterexamples, and formal deduction techniques.
  4. Construct rigorous mathematical and logical proofs using direct proofs, conditional proofs, proof by contradiction, and mathematical induction.
  5. Apply set theory, quantifiers, and formal reasoning techniques to analyze mathematical and computational problems.
  6. Use formal methods to reason about the correctness of programs, including assignments, conditionals, loops, functions, arrays, and program termination.
  7. Develop and apply specifications, preconditions, postconditions, invariants, and loop invariants to verify software behavior and correctness.
  8. Analyze and communicate logical arguments and correctness proofs using appropriate formal notation and terminology.

Course Prerequisites

CIS 200 - C or better

Required Textbooks and Equipment

Textbook will be freely available at textbooks.cs.ksu.edu.

Grading

Grading Modality: Graded (A, B, C, D, F, I)

Course Schedule

This course is an introduction to formal logic. We will learn how to express claims using special syntax, and will learn rules for making deductions about those claims. In the second half of the course, we will apply our formal logic tools towards reasoning about program correctness. In particular, we will cover:

  • Logic puzzles and basic logical reasoning
  • Truth tables
  • Circuits and parse trees
  • Propositional logic, including:
    • Translating English claims into propositional logic
    • Equivalences
    • Valid and invalid arguments in propositional logic; counterexamples
    • Natural deduction rules and proofs in propositional logic
    • Soundness and completeness
  • Sets
  • Predicate logic, including:
    • Quantifiers
    • Translating English claims into predicate logic
    • Equivalences
    • Valid and invalid arguments in predicate logic; counterexamples
    • Natural deduction rules and proofs in predicate logic
  • Types of proofs
    • Conditional proofs
    • Proof by contradiction
    • Proof by induction
  • Programming logic, including reasoning about correctness involving:
    • Assignment operations
    • Conditional statements
    • Loops (including loop invariants)
    • Functions/procedures (including pre- and post-conditions and function specifications)
    • Arrays
    • Global invariants
    • Termination
    • Function specifications in practice

Course Description

A study of concepts and techniques used to produce quality software programs. Object-oriented concepts, models, execution environments, design and testing techniques. Extensive application of these concepts and techniques to the development of non-trivial software programs.

Credit Hours

3 credits

Course Overview

This course is focused on helping you to learn the concepts and skills needed to develop high-quality software. Along the way you will have ample opportunities to practice these skills developing non-trivial software projects. These are not the “baby programs” of early CS coursework, but rather applications that could be used in a production environment.

Accordingly, our goal is not just to write software that compiles without errors, but to develop well-written and maintainable software. This goal demands extra attention to design, documentation, and testing. Additionally, we will explore some of the advanced features of programming languages and tools, as well as other professional tools like code repositories and diagramming software.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, we expect each student to be able to:

  • Create class definitions that 1) utilize encapsulation to organize related data and behavior, 2) prevent uncontrolled internal state changes through information hiding, and 3) allow outside code controlled interactions through message passing.
  • Specify expected object behavior and verify it through creating and executing unit tests against a class definition.
  • Utilize polymorphism in the form of inheritance to minimize code duplication by moving shared fields and methods to a common ancestor.
  • Reason about dynamic dispatch to determine what version of a polymorphic function or method will be invoked at runtime.
  • Utilize industry standard developer tools to develop software reliably and efficiently.
  • Design, implement and test a project of your own design based on the principles covered in this course.

STAT [410/XXX] - Computational Statistics

This document describes a proposed computational statistics course to serve the common computing foundation being developed by the department of computing science. This course is intended to be housed in the Department of Statistics and will be co-designed with computer science faculty. Exact numbering is not yet determined - the STAT 410 number (a previous service course for CS) is used as a placeholder and potential choice.

Also important to note is that between this course, the discrete mathematics sequence, and calculus, the following ABET accreditation requirements must be met: At least 9 semester credit hours (or equivalent) must include statistical inference and modeling, linear algebra, probability, data visualization, and optimization topics.

Course Description

Descriptive statistics and graphical methods; probability, distributions, and simulation, presented through both frequentist and Bayesian perspectives; estimation, hypothesis testing, and resampling; regression and optimization-based modeling. Emphasizes statistical computing and real-world data.

Credit Hours

4 credits

Course Overview

This course serves as the statistical foundation for all bachelor degrees offered by the Department of Computer Science, and is a part of the proposed two-year common computing core coursework. It falls in the fourth semester (Spring of the Sophomore year) providing ample time for students interested in pursuing deeper statistical knowledge to take additional courses, or even double-major in statistics or data science.

The two-year common computing core adopts a spiral curriculum model across the full program, concepts are introduced early and deepened through deliberate repeated exposure at greater depths and multiple contexts across multiple courses. Thus, while this course formalizes statistical reasoning and techniques, students will have had both exposure and practical hands-on experience with statistical concepts and tools the course leverages and builds upon, including:

  • Significant Python programming experience, including consuming data from multiple sources (files, databases, web APIs), programmatically creating web visualizations including charts and graphs, working with Python libraries including Pandas and NumPy.
  • Mathematical foundations from the new discrete math chain, including sets, counting principles, permutations, combinations, binomial coefficients, finite cardinality, and inclusion-exclusion.
  • Foundational linear algebra experience including vectors, matrices, and associated operations (multiplication, transpose, inverse, dot product), systems of linear equations, projections onto a subspace
  • Experience with real-world data sets, including climate and weather data from Kansas Mesonet, sensor data logs from instrumented classroom plants, and historical Kansas population, geographic, educational, and economic data sets.
  • Students will have also computed and displayed descriptive statistics on datasets in earlier programming courses (mean, median, mode, and standard deviation).

Additionally, a focus of the two-year curriculum is to approach topics from multiple lenses encouraging students to develop a deeper, more robust foundational understanding. For this course, that goal would include teaching both Frequentist and Bayesian perspectives side-by-side, often working through the same problems.

Course Goals and Learning Outcomes

Upon completing this course, students should be able to:

  1. Summarize and visualize a dataset using descriptive statistics and graphical methods, evaluating whether a visualization represents data honestly and effectively.
  2. Compute probabilities and describe random variables using discrete and continuous distributions, expectation, and variance.
  3. For a given estimation problem, construct and interpret the solution under both a frequentist framing (point estimates, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) and a Bayesian framing (Bayes’ theorem, posterior estimates, credible intervals), explicitly contrasting what each does and does not establish.
  4. Use Monte Carlo simulation and bootstrap resampling as computational alternatives to closed-form probability and inference methods, including empirical verification of results such as the Central Limit Theorem.
  5. Design and analyze a basic hypothesis test or controlled experiment (e.g., an A/B test), correctly stating the conclusions the method does and does not support and distinguishing correlation from causation.
  6. Fit and interpret simple and multiple linear regression — expressed in matrix/vector form via least squares — and logistic regression for binary classification.
  7. Formulate a model-fitting problem as an optimization problem and apply gradient descent to minimize a loss function.
  8. Evaluate a fitted model’s performance using a train/test split, identifying overfitting and underfitting.
  9. Communicate statistical findings — numerically, visually, and in writing — to technical and non-technical audiences.

Course Requisites

Prerequisites:

  • CIS 116 Introduction to Programming
  • MATH XX2 Counting and Finite Configurations (new discrete math course being developed for the core)
  • MATH 220 Calculus I
  • MATH 551 Matrix Theory or MATH 515 Introduction to Linear Algebra

Course Topics

  • Measures of center, spread, and shape
  • Exploratory data analysis
  • Principles of honest, effective graphical communication; common visualization pitfalls
  • Sample spaces, events, and set-based probability notation
  • Counting-based probability: permutations, combinations, binomial coefficients
  • Random variables; discrete distributions (binomial, Poisson) and continuous distributions (normal)
  • Expectation, variance, joint and conditional distributions
  • Central Limit Theorem
  • Bayes’ theorem; Bayesian vs. frequentist interpretations of probability
  • Monte Carlo simulation
  • Point estimation
  • Confidence intervals
  • Hypothesis testing: parametric tests, p-values, Type I/II error
  • Chi-square tests: goodness of fit, independence
  • Analysis of variance (ANOVA); effect size measures
  • Bootstrap resampling
  • Bayesian credible intervals and posterior estimation
  • Experimental design and A/B testing; correlation vs. causation
  • Simple and multiple linear regression
  • Least squares in matrix/vector form (leans on the linear algebra prerequisite)
  • Optimization: loss functions, gradient descent
  • Logistic regression for binary classification

  • Model evaluation: train/test split, overfitting and underfitting

Course Schedule

Data & Description

  • Week 1: Measures of center, spread, and shape; working with real datasets in Python
  • Week 2: Exploratory data analysis; honest/effective graphical communication and common visualization pitfalls

Probability & Simulation

  • Week 3: Sample spaces, events, set-based probability notation; counting-based probability (permutations, combinations, binomial coefficients)
  • Week 4: Random variables; discrete (binomial, Poisson) and continuous (normal) distributions; expectation and variance
  • Week 5: Joint and conditional distributions; Central Limit Theorem; Monte Carlo simulation (empirically verifying CLT)
  • Week 6: Bayes’ theorem; Bayesian vs. frequentist interpretations of probability — the dual-lens anchor point for the rest of the course

Estimation, Both Lenses

  • Week 7: Point estimation and confidence intervals, paired directly against Bayesian posterior estimation and credible intervals for the same problem

Checkpoint

  • Week 8: Midterm assessment and review, covering description through estimation

Inference & Experimentation

  • Week 9: Hypothesis testing — parametric tests, p-values, Type I/II error
  • Week 10: Chi-square tests (goodness of fit, independence); ANOVA and effect size
  • Week 11: Bootstrap resampling; experimental design and A/B testing; correlation vs. causation

Modeling & Optimization

  • Week 12: Simple and multiple linear regression; least squares in matrix/vector form
  • Week 13: Optimization — loss functions, gradient descent; logistic regression for binary classification
  • Week 14: ANOVA reframed as regression with categorical predictors; model evaluation (train/test split, over/underfitting)

Synthesis

  • Week 15: Final project/presentations — applying descriptive, inferential, and modeling tools (both lenses) to a real dataset; communicating findings to technical and non-technical audiences

Running case studies/datasets threaded across the whole semester rather than siloed by week: education-research scenarios, marketing/business A-B examples, agricultural applications anchored on the Kansas Mesonet dataset already used elsewhere in the core.
