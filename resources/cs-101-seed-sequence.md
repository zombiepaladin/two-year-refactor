# CS-101 Seed Sequence

This is Nathan's worked model for CS-101's paired-introduction pattern — the concrete instantiation of concurrency and networked-computing as cross-cutting norms, interleaved from day one rather than deferred to dedicated later units. It's the standard to match in rigor, not just in topic, when course-designer works out pairings for other blocks. Full weekly breakdown, activities, and assessments still need to be elaborated from this skeleton — this document captures the conceptual sequence and pairing logic, not the finished block content.

## Design notes

- Steps 3–5 deliberately carry no norm pairing yet — vocabulary (variables, memory model, mutability) is built first, then complicated at step 6. This gap is intentional and should stay visible in any elaboration of this sequence, not smoothed over.
- Step 6/7 is where concurrency and networked-computing genuinely converge rather than running in parallel — this is not a coincidence to note once and move past; both norms' fixes (locks/immutability vs. timeouts/retries/idempotency) should be taught as explicit analogs of each other.
- The plant-sensor API's no-auth status is deliberate and temporary for this sequence point — see `plant-sensor-api-roadmap.md` for its future role as a security/auth pairing point. **Its capability-status field is currently `requested`, not `confirmed`** (per that roadmap's own status legend: "not safe to depend on in a block") — this sequence's steps 6–7 already build on it; not a new problem, but worth restating now that it's proposed as CS-101's own opening-block anchor rather than a later touch.
- The HTML/CSS instrument thread's placement (step 1, and again at step 7) is a candidate, not finalized — course-designer should confirm whether it enters this early or is better placed in Foundations of Programming with only a forward-reference here. **See the conflict below — this candidate placement directly reopens a question v1 already closed.**

## Open conflicts with archived v1 (flagged, not resolved)

Reviewed against `content-archive/v1/core-design/blocks/block-1-read.md`, the relevant `design-log.md` passages (2026-07-09 and 2026-07-12 CS-101/CS-102 sessions), and `content-archive/v1/course-designs/block-1/cs-101.md` and `cs-102.md`, which hold a much more detailed, already-built week-by-week CS-101/CS-102 design than the archived block-level summary. (These files were archived to their current path after this comparison was first made — see the resolved note at the end of this section; the content itself is unchanged, byte-identical, only the path moved.) Several substantive conflicts follow. None are resolved here, per instruction.

1. **HTML/CSS at CS-101 reopens a decision v1 already made the other way.** A 2026-07-09 design-log session removed all HTML/CSS content from CS-101 on direct user instruction, with the explicit finding that it was "a misplaced duplication" and "already lived fully in CS-106" (`content-archive/v1/course-designs/block-2/cs-106.md`, which owns the declarative-HTML/CSS-model, accessibility, and browser-as-mini-OS content). This seed sequence's step 1 and step 7 candidate placements put HTML/CSS back into CS-101 without citing that prior resolution. Unclear whether this is an intentional reversal (the paired-norm framing genuinely wants an earlier home for this content) or an independent proposal made without awareness of the 2026-07-09 decision. Needs an explicit call, not a silent pick either way.

2. **Networked-computing content is entirely absent from v1's actual CS-101 build — this is the largest structural conflict.** `content-archive/v1/course-designs/block-1/cs-101.md`'s current week-by-week map (8 weeks, already built in detail) has zero network-boundary content anywhere — no latency framing, no fan-out, no HTTP calls. All networking is deliberately deferred to B2 (CS-106 fetch/API-consumption) and formalized further at B4 (CS-111) and B7 (CS-209 OSI). This seed sequence's steps 1, 2, 6, and 7 add substantial new network-boundary content to CS-101 (latency framing, fan-out of parallel requests, a real HTTP call to the plant-sensor API, HTML-vs-JSON representation duality) — a real scope increase, not a reframing. **This lands on a course already flagged twice (2026-07-09 and 2026-07-12 sessions) as being at or past its density ceiling**, with Weeks 3 and 6 explicitly noted as having "little to no slack." Recommend explicit reconciliation before block content is drafted: either (a) confirm this is an intentional redesign that supersedes v1's density-conscious choice to keep CS-101 network-free, in which case the whole B1 density calculus needs rework from scratch, or (b) scale back the seed sequence's networking depth to fit within what v1's density analysis already found to be a tight fit without it.

3. **Sequencing and content don't map cleanly onto v1's week structure.** v1 defers concurrency naming to Week 7 of 8 (after building vocabulary first) and pairs the stack/heap notional-machine widget with recursion at Week 6. This seed sequence names concurrency immediately at step 2 (right after step 1), treats "memory model" as its own simpler step 4 (a global-variable heap only, no stack/heap-with-recursion combination), and **does not mention recursion anywhere in its 7 steps.** Unclear whether recursion is being cut, deferred to a different block, or simply not yet elaborated in this skeleton (the document's own framing note says it captures "the conceptual sequence and pairing logic, not the finished block content," which would make the omission expected rather than a real cut) — but since v1 spent real, deliberate design effort on recursion as connective tissue between CS-101 and CS-102 ("one function, two angles," cross-referenced in both files), its absence here is worth an explicit confirmation rather than a silent gap.

4. **Two-parallel-courses structure vs. one merged sequence — a real structural ambiguity.** v1's actual architecture is two co-scheduled, separately-credentialed 1-credit courses: CS-101 (mutable/imperative vehicle) and CS-102 (immutable/functional vehicle), synchronized week-by-week (same-week pairing at Week 2, a Week 5 "solve it both ways" checkpoint, a Week 6 shared-recursive-function cross-reference, a Week 7 concurrency companion pass). This seed sequence's step 3 ("variables: imperative assignment + functional pattern matching") reads as if a single sequence covers both paradigms directly, rather than describing parallel content split across two courses. Unclear whether the "steps" in this document are meant to distribute across CS-101 and CS-102 the way v1's weeks do, or whether this sequence assumes a merged single course. This affects how "step" numbers would eventually map onto actual weeks in actual 1-credit courses, and should be resolved before block content is drafted.

5. **Real Python threading vs. v1's deliberately abstract concurrency vehicle.** This seed sequence's step 2 lists "Go co-routines (touch), **Python threads (use)**" and step 6 lists "Python threads" again as a real-code vehicle. v1's CS-101 concurrency seed deliberately avoids any actual threading-library code — it uses an H5P widget (multiple independent "voices"/sequences with nothing shared) and a hand-traced, non-executable lost-update example, explicitly framed as "inspired by" Go goroutines but "not taught as Go syntax," specifically to keep the concept abstract and avoid real threading-library subtleties (the GIL, real race-condition timing, etc.) in a 1-credit intro course. Actually producing/using Python's `threading` module, as this seed sequence's language suggests, is a materially bigger technical commitment than v1's abstract vehicle. Flag before assuming this is a drop-in replacement for v1's widget-based approach.
